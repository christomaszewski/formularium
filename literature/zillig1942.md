# Zillig 1942: how downy mildew epidemics arise

A short German paper from the Mosel advisory service. Its argument: an epidemic on leaves needs
3-4 successive outbreaks, each multiplying the fungus, so the first spray can wait for the first
outbreak. The previous year enters only as one sentence on winter spores. OCR of a whole journal
issue, mostly clean; the two figures are garbled. Line numbers are that file's own. Baldacci 1947
quotes this paper as "Zini (1942)".

## What it holds

**The 3-4 outbreaks rule** (p. 83, l. 22-37).
- Said to be taught by "Beobachtung": an epidemic is helped by weather but "niemals von heute auf
  morgen" caused if the disease was absent or hardly found before (l. 22-25). Several outbreaks
  are needed, each adding to the fungus (l. 25-27).
- **The previous-year statement** (l. 31-34): if the mildew appeared only weakly the year
  before, "sodass sich nur wenige Wintersporen bilden konnten", then 3 to 4 successive outbreaks
  the next spring are needed for mass multiplication.
- Stages (l. 34-37): the first outbreak on quite isolated leaves in susceptible sites, often
  overlooked; the second shows single leaves everywhere; the third many; the fourth very many.
- Asserted. No count of oospores, leaves or outbreaks backs it.

**After a strong year** (p. 84, l. 43-51).
- A first outbreak on many leaves "erscheint möglich" if the mildew was strong the year before
  and many winter spores formed (l. 43-45).
- He has "praktisch ... noch nicht erlebt" it, even in such cases (l. 45-46). His reason (l.
  46-48): many winter spores "offenbar" perish in unfavourable weather, or are destroyed by
  timely digging-under of the old vine leaves in spring.
- Consequence he draws: on less susceptible varieties such as Riesling the first outbreak can
  be waited for, even after a strong year (l. 48-51).
- Asserted from his own experience; the reasons are a guess ("offenbar").

**The practice behind it** (p. 84, l. 64-80). Spraying after the first outbreak is found has
worked for decades: Lüstner (Rheingau, oral), Hepp (Pfalz, letter), Rodrian (Rheinhessen, 20
years), and Zillig himself (Mosel 20 years, the Rhineland nearly 10 years, "ohne einen Fehlschlag"). Reports from others, not data.

**When to spray before the first outbreak** (pp. 84-85, l. 82-108): when no incubation
calendar is used; when no first outbreak is seen just before bloom; on very susceptible
varieties (Müller-Thurgau, Gutedel, Portugieser); when rain is expected to prevent spraying; or
when farm work does not allow a spray within the next incubation period.

**The 1935-36 example** (p. 85, l. 110-128).
- Rule restated for Riesling and other less susceptible varieties: the bloom may be waited out
  while no first outbreak shows, "unter der Voraussetzung, dass der Pilz im Vorjahre kaum auftrat" (l. 110-113).
- 1935: the mildew had "kaum" appeared in the whole Rhenish slate mountains (l. 116-117).
- 1936: first outbreak at the peak of bloom, 22 June (l. 118-119). In a considerable part of the
  area no pre-bloom spray had been applied, and no damage came to Riesling (l. 119-121).
- In the Reichsanstalt's block planting (a susceptible site), the clusters of 12 listed varieties
  were not attacked; in Gutedel and Müller-Thurgau the cluster tips died, a yield loss of about
  20 % (l. 121-128).
- This is the one example in the paper of a weak year followed by a late, weak spring start. One
  case, regional, no counts of previous-year disease; the 20 % is a stated figure for the block.
  The paper does not say how weak 1935 was, or that spores were absent.

**Sudden outbreaks without known foci** (p. 85, l. 130-146). In dry years with a first outbreak
only in the second half of June, many leaves or clusters were rarely seen at once: Neusiedlersee
1937 (Wahl and Voboril, oral), Upper Mosel 1941 (Kieffer). Two explanations offered: overlooked
foci, perhaps neglected nurseries, or wind carrying spores in from a neighbouring area; Zschokke
(Pfalz 1922, from Baden) cited. Opinion.

**Nursery foci, with counts** (pp. 86-88, l. 163-258).
- Local epidemics often start from neglected one-year vine nurseries ("Rebschulen") where old
  leaf litter was not dug under and young leaves were not coppered (l. 163-168). Nurseries
  lie on valley floors among meadows, so there is wetness for infection almost every night
  ("wahre Brutstätten", l. 168-172). He had often found many outbreaks in nurseries while
  the surrounding vineyards were free (l. 172-174).
- **Lieser Niederberg, 1941** (l. 181-222): reported 26 June; a nursery of about 3 m2 (about
  100 vines, value 15 RM) with leaves mostly browned; shoots about 50 cm. Vineyard 10 m away:
  many leaves and clusters infected in a half circle of about 20 m diameter; rarer farther out,
  only single outbreaks at about 100 m (l. 185-194). Kues, about 2 km away, still free on 29
  June (l. 194-202). First infection in the Mosel area was found on one leaf at Klüsserath on
  24 June (l. 203-204). Damage: the crop of a few hundred vines mostly destroyed (l. 209-211).
  Several thousand RM of loss and extra spray against 15 RM of nursery (l. 216-218). Dates and
  distances are observations; "wahrscheinlich" two outbreaks had already happened in the nursery
  (l. 204-206).
- **Kues, Schifferfeld, 7 July 1941** (l. 224-252): a nursery of about the same size, 2.60 m
  from the first row of a cutting planting. Directly adjacent vines: every leaf and cluster
  heavily attacked; at the upper edge, about 60 m away, only single infections (l. 234-238).
  Spread along the lowest rows was not less, probably because of a path (l. 238-242). The adjacent
  hillside still free (l. 244-245).
- Abb. 1 and 2 are said to give the count of infected leaves by position (l. 242-244, 274-279,
  307); the numbers are garbled in the OCR (l. 261-275) and no figures can be read. So the
  distances above are the text's, not the chart's.

**Speed of spread** (p. 88-89, l. 282-315). A Rhineland adviser was surprised by a strong leaf
outbreak on 17 July 1941 after two weak ones (l. 282-286). Zillig: one leaf may form "hunderttausende" of zoospores (l. 287-288), no number sourced; but no explosive spread from one day to the next if the preceding outbreak was slight (l. 298-301).

**Incubation through the season** (pp. 89-90, l. 311-326).
- Mid-May to end June, leaf and cluster incubation falls with warmth from about 18 to 6 days
  (l. 312-314); July-August 5 days (l. 318).
- So about 2 outbreaks in May, about 3 in June, up to 6 a month in July-August with enough
  wet and warmth (l. 319-326). Second half of June often brings the 3rd to 5th outbreak; early
  July after bloom the 4th to 6th (l. 327-331).
- Young berries: incubation 10-14 days (l. 333).
- Statements of what he works from; no table or source.

**Cluster epidemics** (p. 90, l. 341-361): about every 10 years; on the Mosel in 1922, 1932
and 1941 (l. 341-344). Dated cases: 1922 first outbreak 26 June, wet June and July, many
leather berries; 1932 a thunderstorm on 13 July and rain on 30 July; 1941 rain on 12-13 July
and 28 July (l. 344-353). Bloom ended 3 July (1932) and 7 July (1941) (l. 353-355). Stated
as record of events.

**Summary** (pp. 90-91, l. 364-382): on leaves an epidemic comes in the course of 3-4
infections; on fruit set 1-2 leaf outbreaks suffice if rain at high temperature falls soon after bloom and copper is not yet on; so the first spray can wait for the first outbreak unless bloom has come or the exceptions apply; the post-bloom spray must not be delayed.

## Dependence

- No earlier source is named for the 3-4 outbreaks rule or the winter-spore sentence. It rests
  on Zillig's own advisory experience (Mosel, Rhineland).
- Oral and letter reports from Lüstner, Hepp, Rodrian, Wahl, Voboril, Kieffer; Zschokke (1922)
  cited without reference.
- The incubation times are not tied to a source here.
- Downstream: Baldacci 1947 quotes the 3-4 outbreaks line as "Zini (1942)"; Sarejanni 1951 cites it via Baldacci.
- Nothing is fitted or computed.

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

Does the previous year or autumn set next spring's primary infections? Zillig names the previous
*year*, not the autumn: no autumn disease, no oospore count, no winter or spring weather record.
- **Opinion (asserted from experience):** weak year before, few winter spores, so 3-4 outbreaks
  are needed in spring. The mechanism (few spores, so slow build-up) is stated, not shown.
- **Opinion:** after a strong year a mass first outbreak "appears possible" but has not been
  seen, because winter spores die or old leaves are dug under. This says that a strong
  previous year does not reliably give a strong start, which cuts against a simple
  previous-year-sets-spring reading. Digging-under and weather are the named brakes.
- **Observation, one case:** 1935 weak, 1936 first outbreak 22 June and little damage except on two
  very susceptible varieties (about 20 %). Nothing was counted for 1935. The paper gives no
  strong-then-strong year pair with numbers.
- **Observation:** the 1941 nursery foci show that local leaf litter and unsprayed young leaves
  can start a local epidemic early, which is an inoculum-source point, not an autumn-weather one.
- The rule is the origin of the "3 to 4 invasions" line in later Italian work (Baldacci 1947).
