# Shah et al. 2013: weather-window logistic regression for Fusarium head blight epidemics on 527 U.S. wheat observations (analogue crop)

Searches 380 candidate predictors made from hourly temperature, relative humidity and rain in short windows before or after anthesis, and builds 15 logistic models for a major epidemic (severity of 10% or more). No record: a method with measured results.

**Read this first.** Wheat, not a vine. The best-documented design in plant disease for a search over hourly T, RH and rain, but it is scored on a random 70/30 split of observations, several of which share a site-year, so no season is held out. Table 2's AUC is a training-data measure. The selected predictors are collinear; the authors say no single model or variable set was best.

## What it holds

- **Real data** (l. 3, 27): 527 unique observations, 31% major epidemics, from 150 site-years in 15 U.S. states, 1982 to 2009. Several plots per site-year differ in cultivar resistance, flowering date or corn residue, so there are 150 independent weather series, not 527 (arithmetic from the two printed counts). Most observations come from two clusters of years (37% in 2003 to 2005, 30% in 2009, as the reading agent records).
- **Labels are observed** (l. 12): FHB severity and incidence rated in fungicide-untreated plots kept by collaborators; a scientist's rating, not a model's. Major epidemic means severity of 10% or more (l. 3).
- **Inputs** (l. 3, 27): hourly temperature, relative humidity and rainfall recorded at the plot or within 10 km; 80 of 150 hourly series had gaps, filled by multiple imputation (five imputed versions, as the reading agent records). 380 candidate predictors in 5-, 7-, 10-, 14- or 15-day windows before or after anthesis. The models also carry indicators for cultivar resistance, wheat type and corn residue (l. 8).
- **Synthetic data:** none as training data; imputation of missing weather only.
- **Validation** (l. 3): a training set of 70% of the 527 observations, the remainder as test; a random split, no held-out year or state.
- **Skill, score named** (l. 123): across the 15 final models, misclassification rate 0.26 on the training data and 0.34 on the test data; AUC did not exceed 0.9 (Table 2, which lists training-data measures, l. 90). The test misclassification was 19% lower on average than that of the models in the national risk tool (l. 8; relative, same data).
- **Variables** (l. 8, 126, 130): 21 of the 380 predictors were retained, all derivatives of relative humidity or temperature except one rain predictor; none of the dew-point depression or vapour pressure deficit predictors survived; rain predictors were picked less often than RH predictors in the bootstrap.
- **Caveat on mechanism** (l. 8): intercorrelated weather predictors, so "no single model or subset of predictor variables was best".

## Dependence

- Labels observed; no process model computes any label. The baseline is the earlier national models of De Wolf and colleagues (l. 9). The operational models were fitted to a small pre-2006 data set (l. 9). Shares authors with shah2014 and shah2023 and (Shah) with alves2022: a flag only.

## Bearing (2026-10-27)

- **D58 (variable search):** a skill ceiling for hourly T, RH and rain plus three host and residue indicators: test misclassification 0.34 (accuracy about 0.66), AUC at most 0.9, on a random split. Humidity and temperature derivatives won over rain; dew-point depression and VPD added nothing. None of this was tested on held-out years, so a simulator screen is judged here only against a random-split ceiling.
- **Design lesson:** 380 predictors from three hourly variables, two-stage selection with bootstrap, 150 site-years: the scale a real-data check of a screened variable would need.
- **Not shown:** anything on held-out seasons (compare kaundal2006, carisse2021dt) or any synthetic training (D60).
