# Capus 1913: the 1913 invasions, dated by contamination, and soil moisture before budburst

Capus, director of the plant-pathology station at Cadillac (Gironde), dates each mildew
invasion of 1913 back to its rain, and argues that wet soil around budburst, more than the
number of germs, made 1910, 1912 and 1913 early and severe years.

## What it holds

**Method** (l. 13-78, the author's own).
- With helpers in Gironde, the West, Languedoc and elsewhere (l. 39-44), he dated the
  contamination of every 1913 invasion by treatments made on different dates: the first day
  from which spraying no longer helps marks the contamination (l. 25-30).
- At Cadillac, two more methods: a movable shed that shelters one untreated vine from each
  rain in turn (l. 35-65), and potted vines exposed to one rain each, then returned to a
  greenhouse (l. 67-78).

**The invasions of 1913** (author's own observations and dating).
- **First, southern vineyards only** (Hérault, Gard, some Aude; l. 84-94): spots 9-16 May,
  little damage; contamination 9 April, after a fall in temperature from 1 April and daily
  rain 4-9 April (44.30 mm), with 10.3 mm already on 23 March. Budburst in the Hérault was
  "preceded by rain and accompanied by a fall in temperature and rain" (l. 92-94).
- **Second, 28 May** (Gironde, Aude, Hérault, Gard; l. 96-109): contamination 6 May, in
  every region after a fall in temperature and rain in early May; little gravity except in
  flooded Gironde "palus" and the Narbonne plains.
- **Third, 5 June** (Gironde and Narbonne; l. 111-126): contamination from the rainy spell
  of mid-May (floods). In Gironde no earlier invasion had yet produced germs, so this and
  the previous invasion there "have as their origin the germs from the conservation organs.
  They are both primary invasions" (l. 118-121). In the south the conidia of the first
  invasion may have added to the spores "from winter germs" (l. 121-126).
- **Fourth, 13-16 June** (l. 128-197): the most formidable of the year, over the South-West,
  Midi, Burgundy, Mâconnais, Champagne, Loire and West; contamination 2-3 June after a fall
  of temperature at the end of May and rain on 1-3 June. Where the 28 May invasion existed
  it can be the source; where it did not, "a doubt remains" whether wind carried germs from
  afar or whether conservation organs, which on his earlier observations can still give
  primary invasions until about 3 June, gave them (l. 144-153). The same weather favoured
  primary and secondary invasions alike (l. 163-167).
- **Later** (l. 199-244): a fifth on 23-26 June, a sixth from 3 July; in July-August several
  in the South-West, Centre and Burgundy. One interval of 19 days between an appearance and
  the next contamination at Narbonne, the longest he had seen since 1898 (Gironde: 15 days;
  l. 241-244).
- **His conclusion on relations** (l. 246-255): at least two primary invasions in Gironde
  and the Midi; every appearance of spots in 1913 led to a contamination at the first rainy
  spell or isolated rain that followed.

**What makes an invasion severe** (l. 258-323).
- **Opinion drawn from the dates** (l. 276-279): "very important invasions derive from weak
  ones or the reverse"; so it does not seem that more weight should be given to the number
  of germs than to the conditions favouring their development.
- **Observation, spring rain** (l. 279-301): in 1913, as in 1910 and 1912, early and intense
  invasions came with abundant rain wetting the soil a little before budburst and in the first
  phase of growth. Hérault: 98.6 mm from 22 to 31 March, before budburst, then 71.1 mm from
  4 to 9 April; Montpellier 169.9 mm from 21 March to 9 April against 119.2 mm in Gironde;
  Gironde before budburst (1-10 April) 30 mm and during it (10-20 April) 9 mm. So the
  invasions were far less important in the South-West than in the Hérault, except in the
  Garonne "palus" flooded 17-22 May. "The same rainfall on dry soil will not act as on soil
  already saturated" (l. 296-301).
- **Opinion** (l. 303-310): lasting soil moisture can have three effects: favour the
  production of germs from the parasite's conservation organs; raise air humidity; exaggerate
  the host's susceptibility. Floods in mid-May came during incubation, so the first effect
  cannot explain those cases (l. 312-314).
- **Three observations "with the value of an experiment"** (l. 337-389): flooding after the
  contamination of 6 May made the 28 May invasion much heavier in flooded Narbonne plains and
  Gironde palus; ploughing during incubation (Béziers, fourth invasion) and not tying up
  shoots (Aloxe-Corton) also increased attack. He reads each as moisture letting germs
  develop that would have aborted.
- **Conclusions** (l. 395-423): regions with the most intense 1913 invasions had abundant
  April and May rain; elsewhere only flooded land was heavily hit. He had recommended at
  Carcassonne on 17 December 1910 that southern spraying begin earlier; he asks for early
  sprays and repetition at the start of growth in rainy springs and on flooded land.

**Not here:** no record of the previous autumn or winter, no oospore count or germination
test, and no winter-rain rule. The "règle de Capus" is not in this paper (grep: no
"novembre", "automne" or "hiver" except "germes d'hiver" at l. 123 and 126); Baldacci 1947
cites it to Capus 1918 and Capus & Bourdel 1931.

## Dependence

- Own observations and treatments, 1913. His earlier work is cited as "mes observations
  antérieures" (l. 152, no reference) and the 1910 and 1912 years by comparison (l. 262,
  279).
- No other author is cited for any claim; the helpers named at l. 39-44 supplied sites.
  No formulation is computed or tested.

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data, one season:** primary invasions in 1913 are dated to specific rains in April and
  May with soil wetness before budburst; this is spring, not autumn. No autumn record at all.
- **Opinion:** that persistent soil moisture may favour germ production from conservation
  organs (stated as a possibility, with two other effects); that invasion size does not
  follow germ number. This bears against a simple "more autumn disease, more spring germs"
  link, but is argued from how severity followed rain, not from counts.
