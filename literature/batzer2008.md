# Batzer et al. 2008: leaf wetness varies across an apple canopy, and moves a spray warning by up to 30 days

Twelve leaf wetness sensors per tree in Iowa apple orchards show that wetness hours differ by position, most on dew-only nights, and that a cumulative-wetness spray rule shifts by days with the sensor's place.

**Read this first.** Apple (Golden Delicious, 3.5 to 5 m tall semidwarf trees; l. 94-95, 100), not grapevine, and a humid central-Iowa climate where wetness is mostly dew; the warning system is the Brown-Sutton-Hartman rule for sooty blotch and flyspeck, not a grapevine model. The hours are for that canopy and that sensor (painted flat grids at 45 degrees, facing north); the direction of the effect and the placement advice are what carry over. Three-column layout: lines hold fragments of two or three columns (l. 24-39 interleave the abstract with the introduction).

## What it holds

- **Design:** 12 sensors per tree, at four horizontal positions about 0.5 m apart on an east-west transect and three heights, about 3.5 m (upper), 2.5 m (middle) and 1.2 m (lower canopy) (l. 140, 100); four trees per orchard (l. 21, 180). Wetness read every 5 min; daily LWD is the wet time in the 24 h from 1100 to 1100; an hour counts as wet when wetness is detected for 30 min or more (l. 135, 145); periods count only at 4 h or more and end after 2 or more consecutive dry hours (l. 145).
- **Data:** four central-Iowa orchards (Gilbert, Cambridge, Jefferson, Fort Dodge) in 2000, 2001 and 2003; none published. Sensors compared before deployment: no significant hourly difference within four groups of 12 (P 0.9984 to 0.9840), so differences are put down to microenvironment (l. 77).
- **Differences between positions** (abstract and results): the upper, eastern canopy had the longest daily LWD in all data sets, wetted first and dried last (l. 24); the lower, western canopy averaged about 3 h a day less than the top (l. 26). In the single-orchard set the lower western position had 4.5 h less than the upper eastern on no-rain days (dew the only wetness) and about 2.5 h less on rain days (l. 153-156); no-rain days were 72 % of the season (l. 157). The authors give the mean daily upper-to-lower difference as about 2 h, like Germany's (Wittich 1995, cited; l. 327).
- **Height and side:** height and east versus west were significant for daily LWD (P < 0.0001); interior versus exterior was not (P = 0.275), so positions near the trunk were more alike than near the periphery (l. 172). Height differences were 2 h on the outer eastern positions and 4.2 h on the outer western (l. 173).
- **Dew timing and occurrence:** on average the upper eastern canopy wetted 200 min before the lower western (l. 237) and dried about 100 min after it (l. 247). Of 69 dew events (Gilbert 2001), the lower western position had no dew on 16 nights (23 %), parts of the upper canopy on 2 nights (3 %), the middle canopy on 4 to 5 nights (7 %) (l. 238); the abstract gives about 25 % (l. 28).
- **Turf reference:** a sensor 0.7 m over turfgrass, 50 to 100 m from the orchard, saw dew on 86 to 90 % of the nights on which the upper eastern canopy had it (l. 256). Its daily LWD slightly overestimated the top canopy and greatly overestimated the lower (l. 30, 262). R2 of turf LWD against daily LWD in the wettest canopy part was 0.86 to 0.90 and against the driest part 0.55 to 0.77 over five orchard-years (Table 3, PDF p. 5; l. 292-294).
- **Warning output:** the rule advises the second-cover spray when LWD accumulated after the first-cover spray reaches 175 h (l. 151). With LWD from different canopy positions the interval between the first and second cover spray changed by as much as 30 days (l. 33, 282). Each extra hour of mean daily LWD shortened the interval by 4 to 8 days, depending on site-year (l. 312). Moving a lower-canopy sensor by only 0.5 m changed the second-cover timing by 7 days or more (l. 315). LWD from the wettest parts gave conservative advice that still saved at least one spray against a 12-day calendar programme (l. 271).
- **Authors' placement advice:** the system was developed in North Carolina and Kentucky with lower-canopy sensors, which in Iowa is the most variable place (l. 319); growers often use one sensor per orchard or block (l. 315). LWD hours of the wettest part come consistently from a sensor at the top of the canopy or 0.7 m over nearby turf (l. 371); an extra-canopy sensor may replace a within-canopy one for reliability (l. 36-37).
- **Climate caveat:** the dew pattern differs from a semiarid Australian orchard where the canopy centre was wettest (Penrose and Nicol 1996, cited), put down to rain-driven wetness in arid and dew-driven in humid climates (l. 320).

## Bearing (2026-10-22)

- **What it answers:** how far a wetness reading depends on where the sensor sits, the question for Agrarium's placement pilot (D48) and Cooptera E1 and E9. Within one tree the daily difference was 2 to 4.5 h, larger on dew-only days, and one hour of daily LWD moved a cumulative-wetness rule by 4 to 8 days. A position error is therefore a real error term for a sensor menu, and it is largest where nights are dewy.
- **Takeable:** the direction (top and east wettest, lower and west driest; heterogeneity larger when wetness is dew) and the rule of thumb that an open, 0.7 m turf or top-of-canopy sensor tracks the wettest part (R2 0.86 to 0.90) but not the driest (0.55 to 0.77). Not takeable: the hours for vines, whose canopy, row and trellis differ, or for another climate; Catalonia's dew regime is not tested.
- **Not shown:** no vineyard, no optimisation of sensor positions, no sensor-error comparison across sensor types, and no test of the advice on held-out orchards. The warning system is one cumulative-LWD rule, so the 30-day shift does not transfer to a model with infection thresholds by temperature. Kind of result: field measurement at four orchards over three seasons, with a simulation of one rule.
