# Müller-Thurgau 1911: infection of the vine, and the first oospores in artificially infected leaves

Infection experiments on potted vines under glass: conidia on the underside of leaves infect,
conidia on the upper side did not. At the end, an unexpected result: many oospores in the
small, early-dead infection spots of older leaves.

## What it holds

- **Setup** (l. 2256-2268, the summary, author's own): fruiting potted vines of white Gutedel
  and later blue Burgunder under glass with plenty of light and air; fresh conidia in water
  drops on ink-circled spots; humid infection boxes against drying. (The place is not named
  in the excerpt; Switzerland is inferred from the author and from the journal references.)
- **Underside against upper side** (l. 2270-2275, 2296-2301): at 2 days in the box, 219
  sites on upper sides gave no infection, 227 on undersides gave 16 after 8 days and 60 after
  29 days. In a second series, with 6 days in the box (434 upper-side sites: none; 581 underside: 359, 62 %, visible
  by day 6 and 487 by day 27, the OCR percentage cut at "84"). Conclusion at l. 2197-2201: no
  infection from the upper side even in the most favourable conditions.
- **Oospores** (p. 693, l. 2153-2195, author's own observation and inference): examining
  leaves collected after the end of the experiment, "in the small, early-dead infection spots
  of the older leaves, in which often only a few and partly stunted conidiophores had
  formed, numerous oospores had formed". Since the fungus could not long live in the already
  dead and dried tissue, they must have formed "soon after the spread of the mycelium".
  Opinion (l. 2160-2166): probably the low water content, which hindered lateral spread and
  abundant conidial formation, made the fungus form oospores; in younger leaves, whose
  infected parts lived longer and carried dense conidiophores, no oospores were found, even
  in the spots that finally died.
- **First record** (l. 2168-2174): "to my knowledge this is the first time that oospores of
  Plasmopara viticola were observed in artificially infected leaves"; in Gutedel and
  Burgunder alike. Their distribution in the small spots varied: sometimes mainly at the
  edge, often over the whole area, often in small groups.
- **The count, as printed** (l. 2184-2195): the figure shows a part of a small dead spot at
  100x magnification, cleared with chloral hydrate and mounted in Canada balsam; "the few finer
  leaf veins and the ca. 80 oospores (in the 0.38 mm² [OCR "qrpm"] leaf area drawn) are easy
  to recognise". The printed numbers are about 80 oospores and 0.38 mm². The density of about
  210 oospores per mm² is computed here (80 / 0.38), not printed; later authors cite
  200 per mm² for him (Grünzel 1961, as in [grunzel1961](grunzel1961.md)).
- **Not in the excerpt:** any statement on overwintering, germination, or a next-year effect;
  the summary points after number 2 (they continue past l. 2301).

## Dependence

- Own experiments. Cites Ruhland and von Faber for earlier work on the side of infection
  (l. 90-94 and 424), done on young seedlings, whose leaves may behave differently from those
  of older vines (his remark).
- No formulation computed or tested.

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data, one set of potted vines:** oospores can form within the same season as the
  infection, shortly after mycelial spread, in small early-dead spots of older leaves; one
  spot drawing shows about 80 oospores in 0.38 mm². So oospore formation need not wait for
  autumn.
- **Opinion:** that a low water content of the dying tissue induces oospore formation.
- No field overwintering, no autumn weather and no spring infection measured: nothing here
  tests whether the autumn before sets the next spring's primary infections.
