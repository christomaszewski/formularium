# Cabaleiro et al. 2022: leafroll spread in one plot, with a mealybug vector arriving

Spread of GLRaV-3 in one 1989 plot at Meaño (Pontevedra) was slow for 25 years. The rate
rose after the mealybug Planococcus ficus was found in 2013, and the authors link the rise to
that vector (l. 47-51). Survey data cover the Galician wine areas.

## What it holds

- **Vineyard survey, Galicia** (l. 24, 27-28): mealybugs were in 2.2 % of vineyards in the
  1990s, 15 % in 2004 and 80 % in 2016.
- **Plot, 30 years** (l. 35-37, 39-41): the disease progress curve was linear for 25 years, at
  about 0.6 % of plants a year. Since 1992 more than 82 % of infected plants were on the west
  side of the plot, as were 84 % of new infections.
- **After 2013** (l. 47-51): a small Pl. ficus infestation in 2013. Between 2014 and 2016 the
  infection rate rose to more than 21 % a year (l. 50-51). The fitted rate was 14.1 % a year for
  2014-2019 (l. 314-317).
- **Model fits** (Table 4, l. 364-371): over 1992-2019 the exponential fit has the higher R2
  (0.809 against 0.682 for linear, l. 364-365). The paper uses two linear phases instead: slope
  0.0062 (0.6 % a year) with R2 0.984 to 2014 (l. 369), and slope 0.1414 (14.1 % a year) with
  R2 0.976 for 2014-2019 (l. 371).

## Dependence

- The plot is the one described in Cabaleiro et al. 2008 and Cabaleiro & Segura 1997 (l. 139-140).
- The curve models follow Madden et al. 2017 (l. 150-152). The dispersion index follows Campbell
  and Madden (l. 182).

## Bearing (2026-10-20)

- Strand t6 (regional spread) and t7 (mealybug vector). A spread model could take the two phases:
  slow spread with no effective vector, then a faster rise once the vector is present. The west-side
  pattern (l. 39-41) is a check on any spatial kernel.
- One plot, one virus, and no weather or vector counts in the model. Not read in full.
