# Baldacci 1947: six seasons of downy mildew in the Oltrepò Pavese, and what sets the first invasions

Observatory records of 1941-46 from the Oltrepò Pavese (Lombardy), with the author's own
germination tests on last year's leaf fragments. The source of the "three 10" primary-infection
rule as the tools cite it. Old OCR, partly garbled; where a number could not be read, it is
said so.

## What it holds

**The "three 10" rule (stated as known, not as his finding).**
- **Rule** (l. 513-524): primary invasion needs an air minimum of about 10 °C or more, at
  least 10 mm of rain in 48 h, and shoots of 5-10 cm. Baldacci calls it a mnemonic and writes
  that the conditions "sono note"; no source is named for the rule itself.
- **Leaf size, cited** (l. 526-537): Ravaz (1914) held that infection can start at budburst;
  Müller-Thurgau (1911) that very small leaves are not infected, only at about 5 cm
  diameter; Müller (1922) that leaves of 2 x 3.2 cm on shoots of 6-7.5 cm are infected; Müller
  (1934) that smaller leaves can be infected in warmer months.
- **Leaf size, his own** (l. 546-597): greenhouse infections with a drop of conidia
  suspension. The area covered by mould grows with leaf area (l. 574-589; most area figures
  garbled in the OCR, only 1.08 cm² for 12-16 cm² leaves, 1.41 for 16-20 and 1.20 for 28-32
  are legible). He concludes that field primary invasions succeed only on leaves of 6-8 cm²
  (l. 595-597); shoot length is a rough guide only. Chlorotic leaves and old tough leaves are
  not infected (l. 601-605); leaf water content did not track infection (l. 615-627).

**His observations of the first appearance ("evasione primaria"), by year** (his observers
did not measure rain in mm; rain days are used instead, l. 668-671).
- **1941** (l. 642-709): primary spots at Canneto 27 May and Oliva Gessi 25 May. Table II
  (l. 647-662; the Oliva Gessi first-decade row is unreadable): Canneto minimum
  temperatures by decade of May 7.0, 9.3 and 9.9 °C, with 1, 3 and 5 rain days; Oliva Gessi
  9.5 °C (2 days) and 10.3 °C (4 days) in decades II and III. Pavia rain 7.7, 53.7 and
  51.9 mm for the three decades (l. 672-675). Rivanazzano 14 June and Casteggio 23 June;
  he judges low temperature, with "buona probabilità", the limiting factor there
  (l. 676-698).
- **1942** (l. 711-754): primary in the third decade of May at most stations, June at the
  rest, with minima already well above 10 °C; the 10 mm rain threshold was met only in
  decade II (Table IV: Voghera 42.0 mm, Canneto 10.0 mm; decade-I Voghera figure
  unreadable). Table III's columns are too garbled to read.
- **1943** (l. 756-769): minimum reached 10 °C in decade I; heavy rain on 2, 3, 4 and 6 May
  (Table VI as printed: Stradella "107,60" and Pavia 135.0 mm in decade I, probably 107.6;
  nothing after); conditions met, but the primary invasion appeared in few places.
- **1944** (l. 801-826): epidemic very weak; primary only at Canneto and Montù Beccaria in
  the third decade of May, mean minimum 10 °C; May rain scarce (22.6 mm Stradella, 16.6 mm
  Voghera).
- **1945** (l. 828-833): sporadic spots; no epidemic all season, although May rain was not
  scarce (Voghera 52.6 mm, Stradella 33.9 mm).
- **1946** (l. 832-833, 1084-1105): May rain still heavier; primary invasions very late, at
  high temperatures. Cassino Po: primary spots in the 30 April to 29 May period with
  111.5 mm (Table IX, l. 1197-1199), then oil spots and mould in June; by the end of June
  98 % of clusters were attacked on untreated plants (l. 1092-1094). Rivanazzano: nothing in
  May despite 15 rain days, first spots 7 June, secondary from 22 June, contained
  (l. 1099-1105).

**Soil temperature for germination** (l. 838-900).
- **Cited:** Arens (1929) gives a laboratory minimum of 13 °C; "Italian scholars" (unnamed)
  say it can happen below 10 °C air temperature.
- **His data:** Canneto, May 1942 (Table VII, l. 853-875): soil at 5 cm at 12:00 was 0 to
  2.2 °C above air, with two exceptions (one a -5.4 °C reading he marks "errore ?"); primary
  spots on 22 May.
  Same for June (not printed). He infers germination at about 12-13 °C, possibly 10.5-11 °C
  when the air is 9-9.5 °C (Cassino, 1943). The summary (l. 2301-2306) gives soil 12-18 °C,
  seldom 11 °C.

**Inoculum: what he says about the year before** (l. 904-1111).
- **Premise** (l. 292-298, the preface to the calendar): the first rain after the air
  minimum reaches 10 °C makes infection possible from "the oospores left with last year's
  dead leaves". Stated, not tested.
- **Conditions are necessary, not sufficient** (l. 907-921): the weather factors do not
  explain every appearance of the primary invasion nor its course; they leave out the
  number of reproductive organs, "inoculo", which he takes as the oospores in the soil in
  spring.
- **Cited, unnamed** (l. 925-932): work "outside Italy in the last 20 years" (none in
  Italy) gives a correlation between oospore number and germination capacity and the
  intensity of the secondary invasions: few and slowly germinating oospores are followed by
  up to 3 or 4 secondary invasions of little practical interest, even with abundant May rain.
- **Cited, Zillig 1942** (l. 934-954; the text prints "Zini", the reference list at l. 2493
  gives "Zillig H. (1942), Wie entstehen Plasmopara-Epidemien? Z. Pflanzenkr. 52, 83", and
  l. 1076 and 2376 print "Zillig"): a quotation that mildew epidemics are favoured by
  weather but cannot arise overnight if the disease is not already present; several
  successive infections are needed, each multiplying the spreading organs. "If in the
  previous year downy mildew was only weak, so that the formation of oospores was scant, 3 or
  4 successive invasions are needed in spring" for a mass spread: the first sporadic and
  often unnoticed, the second a few leaves, the third many, the fourth very many. Footnote 4
  (l. 986-989), unattributed: oospore germinability can last up to a year and a half, so
  oospores can cause primary infections even two years after their formation; "scarce
  germination" is not the same as loss of germinability. Opinion, quoted; no data given.
- **Cited, Schad 1943** (l. 956-965), centre of France, 1938: low percentage and long
  duration of oospore germination; rain from 15 May to 5 June caused only a few primary
  invasions in the most favourable places; rain from 30 June to 10 July caused a new, more
  general invasion; secondary invasions were notable only in August and September.
- **His own, spring 1941, Canneto** (l. 967-1025): leaf fragments still lying in the soil of
  a vineyard (Zucchini's), "nothing having been prepared the year before", germinated in the
  laboratory by Arens's method. Germination was very slow and very rare. The epidemic was
  slow: primary 27 May, mould from 2 June, the next spots only 16 days later (18 June),
  the third on 24 June; 6 rain days between the first and second. He could not relate the
  epidemic to oospore numbers, "the number of counts being too small" (l. 1023-1025).
- **His own, autumn 1941 to 1942, Canneto** (l. 1027-1048): leaves collected at leaf fall
  and put in a hollow in the same vineyard's soil under a wire mesh; sampled from April.
  Oospores "numerous" in the tissue, germination fast, about 6-7 days in April and 4 days in
  May. The 1942 epidemic was more intense than 1941's: primary 22 May, then secondary
  appearances on 9, 25 and "31" June (printed; June has 30 days) and 7 July, with fewer rain
  days (one rain day against 6 in the 1941 period; mean 8:00 relative humidity 82.3 % in 1941
  and 81.4 % in 1942). He finds the difference hard to relate to weather and puts it down to
  the oospores' behaviour. No counts are printed; no autumn 1940 or 1941 disease record is
  given.
- **His own, 1946** (l. 1049-1071): leaf fragments sent from Rocca de' Giorgi and Cassino Po
  (buried per Ravaz 1912) showed "scarce" germination, directly and in tests at variable
  temperature, which made him predict weak first attacks. The epidemic then bore this out
  "despite the May and June rain": the secondary invasions needed 3 successive infections
  (Table VIII) before the 4th spread in mass. No germination counts are printed. Table VIII
  (l. 1131-1170) is the Montù Beccaria observatory log, not the sites tested: minimum 10 °C
  reached 24 April 1946; "qualche macchia d'olio" 9 May and "rare" spots 15 May (both
  marked primary); mould and new spots 27 May (secondary); mould in growing quantity 7 June;
  new mould spots 15 June; a "forte invasione" on a day printed only as "2" (OCR cut); many
  spots on 13 July. Temperatures and rain notes are in the table (garbled in places).
- **Reading across** (l. 1073-1082): the course resembles what the cited authors saw, but
  incubation is shorter and mass invasion earlier than in northern climates (Zillig: Baden,
  late June to July; Schad: Aug-Sept in central France); May and June rain did not raise
  attack intensity in the Oltrepò.
- **His conclusion** (l. 1106-1111): development of the epidemic is tied to "inoculo": number
  of oospores and speed of germination.

**Capus's winter-rain scheme and his test of it** (l. 1113-1285).
- **Cited:** Capus (1918, Ann. Min. Agric., Eaux et Forêts, fasc. 48) and then Capus &
  Bourdel (1931, "Pluie et mildiou") showed that primary invasions are stronger and earlier
  when winter rain was abundant (l. 1115-1211). Capus's scheme (l. 1215-1222): case 1,
  abundant rain November to April: rain in May-June gives a strong infection, none in
  May-June almost none; case 2, scarce rain November to April: rain in May-June gives a weak
  invasion, none almost none. Baldacci prints it without a source page.
- **His test, Table X** (l. 1267-1285), Voghera, Nov-Apr and May-June rain (mm), with
  "foreseen by the scheme" against "real":

  | Season | Nov-Apr | May-Jun | Foreseen | Real |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1940-41 | 416.0 | 152.0 | strong | scarce |
  | 1941-42 | 268.6 | 76.5 | weak | mediocre or scarce |
  | 1942-43 | "2540" (254.0?) | "42419" (unreadable) | weak | scarce |
  | 1943-44 | 162.6 | 77.6 | almost none | almost none |
  | 1944-45 | 253.8 | 68.0 | almost none | almost none |
  | 1945-46 | 428.0 | 103.6 | strong | fair, only late |
  | means, 1875-1884 (footnote 2: Anfossi 1911, Ligurian region) | 333.6 | 145.3 | | |
  | means, 1928-1941 (footnote 3: Po hydrographic office) | 353.6 | 140.0 | | |

  The 1942-43 decimal point and the May-June figure are lost in the OCR.
- **Result** (l. 1228-1234): no agreement in 1940-41 and 1945-46, when winter and spring
  rain was above the mean; agreement in the other years, which were dry. His inference:
  the ecological conditions are necessary but not sufficient. He adds that Müller & Sleumer
  (1934) made a like comparison at some German stations and reached the same conclusion
  (not seen here).
- **His reading** (l. 1236-1255): the epidemic depends in the end on the pathogen's
  "inoculo"; in the years examined "those biological factors were just what was missing", so
  no pronouncement is possible; the behaviour of oospores in April-May is "the best warning"
  of how the epidemic will go, so a warning service should add a laboratory germination test
  of last year's leaf fragments to its observatory network.
- **Summary line** (l. 2330-2334): in general a correlation between scarce winter-spring
  rain and a weak epidemic, but abundant rain is not sufficient unless a high inoculum is
  present.

## Dependence

- **Rule "three 10":** no source printed for the rule; leaf-size cases from Ravaz 1914,
  Müller-Thurgau 1911, Müller 1922 and 1934.
- **Oospore germination:** method from Arens 1929 (laboratory), pit technique from Ravaz
  1912 (l. 1054); minimum 13 °C from Arens.
- **Inoculum statements:** Zillig 1942 (printed "Zini"), Schad 1943, and unnamed foreign
  work; the 18-month germinability footnote has no source.
- **Capus's scheme:** Capus 1918 and Capus & Bourdel 1931, both cited, neither seen by
  Baldacci in the text; the test is his own.
- **Own data:** observatory records of 1941-46 (his observers' notes), Canneto leaf-fragment
  tests in 1941, 1942 and 1946. Nothing here is fitted or computed.

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

Does the autumn before a season set next spring's primary infections? Baldacci gives no
record of any autumn: no late-season disease, no oospore count in autumn, no autumn weather.
What he does give, and how far it is data:
- **Data, thin:** in two springs at one site (1941, 1942) germination of last year's leaf
  fragments was slow and rare in the year of a slow epidemic and fast and plentiful in the
  year of a stronger one; in 1946, "scarce" germination at two sites was followed by a
  slow epidemic. Nothing is counted; the 1941 and 1942 tests used different leaf material
  (leftover fragments against leaves buried at leaf fall), so they are not a clean pair.
  The leap from fragments of one vineyard to the district is his.
- **Data, six seasons:** winter rain (Nov-Apr) at Voghera does not predict severity: two
  wet winters (1940-41, 1945-46) gave little disease, the four drier ones agreed with the
  scheme. This is a counter-example to Capus's winter-rain rule on a single station, with a
  qualitative "real" column.
- **Opinion, cited:** a weak year before needs 3-4 spring invasions before mass spread
  (Zillig 1942 as quoted); germinability can last 1.5 years (unsourced footnote).
- **Opinion, his:** that the spring oospore test is the best early warning; he says himself
  the inoculum factor "was missing" in the years observed, so the link from the previous
  autumn is inferred, not shown.
- Where the 3-10 rule enters the tools: the leaf-size and soil-temperature data above are
  what the rule rests on in this paper; its three numbers are asserted as known, then
  checked against 1941-46 dates in tables that are partly unreadable.
