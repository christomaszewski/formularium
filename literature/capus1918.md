# Capus 1918: a dry winter, and spring mildew only on wet soil (Cadillac)

A page and a half of Capus's own station report for 1918. It is not the 1918 forecasting report
that later authors cite for the November-April rain rule; that report (Annales fasc. XXXVIII,
1919; Baldacci 1947 prints it as "Ann. Minist. d'Agric., Série des Eaux et forêts, f. 48") is not
on the Internet Archive and was not found. Line numbers are those of the excerpt file, section
[A], lines 3-41 (the header, which names the item, is line 2). The text breaks off after
the Black rot section ("Puis la", l. 41). OCR is mostly clean.

## What it holds

**Mildew, 1918** (l. 7-24).
- Capus studied invasions "dans leurs rapports avec les circonstances atmosphériques" (l. 7-8).
- Low water content of the great majority of vineyards at budburst led him to expect that
  the spring invasions, "si redoutables pour les grappes", would not occur (l. 9-11).
- Result (l. 11-13): mildew did not rage in spring in nearly all vineyards, but showed with
  intensity wherever the soil was wet.
- The Garonne flooded in 1918; the palus vines on both banks were submerged for several days
  (l. 13-15). In all flooded vineyards treated outside the favourable dates, mildew appeared on
  6 June, and "n'a paru que sur ceux-là"; clusters strongly invaded (l. 15-17).
- He traced that 6 June invasion to a contamination on 22 May: rain from 20 h to 8 h next
  morning with pauses, 3 mm in all (l. 18-21).
- Dryness persisted afterwards, so no other invasions in 1918 (l. 23-24).
- Data in the sense of dated observation: one year, one station region, rain figure (3 mm) and dates given; no
  counts of vineyards, leaves or oospores; no winter rain figures. The words "sécheresse de
  l'hiver" appear only in the Black rot paragraph (l. 28). For mildew the cause named is low
  soil water at budburst, in the flooded versus the dry vineyards.

**Black rot contrast** (l. 26-36). The dry winter had no effect on Black rot invasions in its
foci; the water-retaining soils there got enough from April rain (l. 28-32). Wet periods of
19 April and 26 May at first leaves gave invasions at the end of May and 6 June in the Gers,
Gironde and Charentes (l. 32-35); the biggest on leaves in Gironde since 1903 (l. 35-36). He
had never seen such a contrast in June: many Black rot spots in the foci, not one mildew spot
in the same places (l. 38-40).

## Dependence

- Own observation, Cadillac station, 1918. No source is cited in the excerpt.
- The rule that November-April rain sets the first invasion is not stated here; it is
  attributed to Capus 1918 by Schad 1936 and Baldacci 1947 (see schad1936, baldacci1947). This
  excerpt supports only a soil-water link in one dry-winter year.
- Related Capus items cited elsewhere and not obtained: L'évolution et le traitement du Mildiou en
  1915 (Revue de viticulture, p. 157, 1916); Traitement des maladies de la vigne (1910). The
  1923 Paris plant-pathology congress says Capus set out the forecasting methods in the
  Annales of the Direction générale des Eaux et Forêts, year 1918 (the Ravaz report is in the
  1919 volume of that series).

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

Does the autumn or previous year set next spring's primary infections? Not the autumn: this
excerpt has no autumn or previous-year disease, and no oospore data.
- **Observation, one year:** a dry winter and dry soil at budburst went with no spring mildew
  except on wet (flooded) soil, where it came after one 3 mm rain on 22 May. That points to
  soil water at budburst and to rain on the day of contamination, not to stored inoculum.
- **Not shown:** that the dry winter itself, as distinct from the dry spring, was the cause.
  Capus says low water at budburst; the winter-rain rule needs the 1918 report.
- Eggs (oospores) are not mentioned in the excerpt.
