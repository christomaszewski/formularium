# Ravaz & Verge 1913: the first germination of the winter spores

How the oospores ("spores d'hiver") of Plasmopara viticola were made to germinate in
Montpellier in spring 1911, what they produce, how fast, and one test of how long they last.

## What it holds

- **Earlier failures, cited** (l. 10-33, 47-121): Farlow could not see germination; Cornu
  neither, and he reasoned that it should give sporiferous filaments in air and perhaps
  zoospores under water; Millardet thought they germ like those of Cystopus; Fréchou saw them
  empty into zoospores in water after 5-6 days but usually after over a month, by a long
  tube, never conidia; Viala and Richon saw zoospores (10 to 18 per oospore, Viala);
  Prillieux saw one or several germ tubes becoming conidiophores.
- **Material** (l. 125-155, author's method): at the end of the season, just before the
  first frosts, leaves with "mosaic" spots are picked, since they usually hold the most
  winter spores; "the smaller the mosaic elements, the more numerous the spores" (counting
  under low-power microscope is possible, "if one wants"; no count printed). Pieces are laid
  outdoors in the vineyard on the soil surface, held with stones or glass rods, in ordinary
  conditions of moisture and heat, and used as needed.
- **Method** (l. 164-173): in winter the dormant spores "do not evolve" outdoors; to follow
  them, washed pieces of 1-2 mm are put in watch glasses or Petri dishes with just enough
  sterilised rain water to cover, in an oven or heated greenhouse.
- **Result** (l. 175-178): germination first obtained in spring 1911, with a student
  (Stoyanovich); first reported to the Pamplona congress, July 1912.
- **Form** (l. 180-245): the oospore puts out a thin, sinuous mycelial filament (2-3 um) that
  gives a conidium like the summer one but larger (mean 33 x 23 um, l. 244-245); one oospore
  may give two or three filaments. One conidium reached full size in 2 h 55 min (l. 229-233).
- **Gregory cited** (l. 247-346): Gregory (Phytopathology, Dec 1912, read late) put leaves
  outdoors in a wire box, brought some in February 1912, found first germination on 29 March,
  then nearly daily to 16 April and some to the end of May; he saw conidia on a
  promycelium, not zoospores directly from the oospore.
- **Speed** (l. 407-425, author's observation): early in the season germination needed 2-3-4
  days in the watch glasses; later "2-1 days" (printed); pieces put in water on the evening
  of 25 March gave well-formed conidia, some already emptied of zoospores, next morning; in
  the best conditions double germination "should not need more than a few hours".
- **Water** (l. 431-443): germination needs plenty of water; fragments half-dipped (water by
  capillarity), wrapped round a wet bulb, or soaked and put in a saturated bell all failed,
  while controls germinated. Opposes Cornu's idea that oospores give zoospores in water.
- **How long they last** (l. 449-458): leaves with winter spores kept outdoors on sand since
  autumn 1911 were put several times in water; "we never saw macroconidia appear", so the
  spores "would lose their germinating faculties very quickly. But is it always so?" The date
  of these tests is not in the excerpt; the OCR here is partly garbled.
- **Temperature** (l. 499-513): greenhouse nights 9-10 °C, days 25-28 °C; a cellar at a
  constant 12.5 °C from 27 March gave a macroconidium on 29 March and many by 2 April;
  a fruit store at 11 °C from 27 March (result cut off). The systematic study was put off
  to the next year.
- **Not printed in the excerpt:** oospore counts per area, a link to autumn disease or winter
  weather.

## Dependence

- Cites Farlow 1876, Cornu 1882, Millardet (no reference given), Fréchou (C. R., 9 Feb 1885), Viala 1889,
  Prillieux 1883 and Gregory 1912 for earlier work; the 1910-11 germination series, the water
  tests and the one-year viability test are the authors' own.
- Gregory's results are quoted from his paper (Gregory is also in this repository as
  gregory1915).

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data:** oospores from leaves picked in autumn stayed dormant outdoors through winter and
  germinated in a few hours to days once warmed and soaked in March-April; germination was
  faster later in spring. So oospores formed in autumn are available for spring infection:
  this is the mechanism, observed, not a measure of how much inoculum a given autumn leaves.
- **Data, one test:** leaves from autumn 1911 kept outdoors did not germinate when tested,
  with no date and no count: a hint that viability may be short (Baldacci 1947 quotes up to
  1.5 years without a source).
- Nothing here ties late disease, autumn or winter weather to the next spring's primary
  infections.
