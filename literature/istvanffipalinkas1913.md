# Istvánffi & Pálinkás 1913: Hungarian studies of mildew, and what they say of winter spores

A monograph from the Royal Hungarian Central Ampelological Institute: infection, incubation,
the fungus's structure, and (pp. 84-86) a short section on winter spores and on mycelium in
buds. Winter spores are found "rarely"; the authors still take them as the source of the
first spots of spring and predict their appearance from weather.

## What it holds

**Winter spores: where and when found** (p. 85-86, l. 4830-4886; the authors' own
observations).
- Despite great care they found winter spores only rarely. The earliest first signs of their
  development were seen in mid-July (l. 4831-4832); on 27 July 1910 on leaves sent from
  Kisvejke they were already brown, so formed much earlier (l. 4832-4834); also in August
  (the printed years "1901" and "191f" at l. 4835-4836 are OCR-uncertain), and on 11 October 1911 on
  young green shoots, ripe and brown (l. 4836-4837). Berlese is cited as having found them in
  green shoots (l. 4838). In ripened canes, Istvánffi ("j'ai constaté", refs 40 and 41)
  saw brown oospores in the bark in winter 1903-04 (l. 4839-4840).
- In autumn 1910 they found winter spores in the lees of musts and wines at Kecskemét and
  Nyíregyháza, "a large number" in the Kecskemét wine lees (l. 4846-4848), which shows they
  can form in berries in numbers, and may escape from berries later and with more difficulty
  than from leaves (l. 4849-4852; their reasoning).
- **Their conclusion** (l. 4854-4856): winter spores "are not formed after the cooling of
  autumn, but their development begins already at mid-summer". They ask whether periodic cold spells in
  August play a part, and name it as worth studying (l. 4856-4858).
- **A negative survey** (l. 4859-4865): a circular at the end of August 1911 to 800 places
  brought about 2000 mildewed leaves from 125 places; 800 spots, cleared in chloral hydrate,
  held no winter spores.
- Young oospores of 11 August (1911) were 25-35 um across, between palisade cells (l. 4867-4869).
- **Opinion** (l. 4874-4886): oospores formed in berries and in late autumn shoots "can also
  play a role in the spring invasions", but it is doubtful whether control can be aimed there
  (picking up bunches and removing late shoots cannot be counted on); the delay of the first
  invasions "raises many important questions linked to the biology of winter spores", to be
  studied now that weather relations are clear.

**Winter spores and the first spots** (the authors' inference from weather and dates).
- First spots usually appear on the lower leaves; "these infections are caused by winter
  spores when there are only 3 or 4 leaves on the young shoot (May)" (p. 36, l. 1976-1978).
  Stated, no source and no germination test.
- **1911** (p. 44, l. 2414-2452): first spots seen 5-6 June; about 800 latent spots per
  arpent (5755.2 m²) appeared on 6 and 10 June (l. 2427-2428). That number implies an earlier
  first invasion, "when mildew woke from its winter rest" (l. 2429-2430), so conditions of
  30 April to 13 May are chosen. April up to the 12th was rainy but the winter spores "could
  not germinate in that time because it was too cold (there was snow too)"; then dry until
  30 April; from 30 April a rainy, relatively warm spell (126 mm on 3 May as printed, 24.4 mm
  on 7 May; night humidity 90 %, day 70 %); first invasion about 5 May; spots stayed latent
  to the rain of 20 May. The incubation was 16-18 days.
- **1912** (p. 49-50, l. 2783-2815): May was very dry; they judge the heat and moisture
  needed for winter-spore germination to be met only on 17 May, so first spots were predicted
  for about 2 June from a 15-18 day incubation; two spots were found on 5 June in their
  3 arpents of experimental field (l. 2788-2797); at Kecskemét, after 32.8 mm on 16 May, the
  first spots appeared 2 June (l. 2801-2808). They inspected daily from 20 May and found no
  more than the two (l. 2810-2815).
- **Practice** (p. 90, l. 5071-5083): to protect against spring primary infection, note rains
  of 10 mm or more from the end of April to mid-May, add 15 or 18 days, and that is when oil
  spots from winter-spore infections may appear; with minima at 10 °C (as printed) infection
  is not to be feared. A caution at p. 92 (l. 5155): from the first spots one cannot conclude
  the region is threatened; that depends on rain, on whether conidia are present, or on
  whether temperature favoured the germination of winter spores.

**Mycelium over summer, autumn and winter** (author's observations unless said).
- **Summer to autumn** (p. 40-41, l. 2226-2291): in oil spots under two months of drought,
  the mycelium stayed alive; conidiophores formed in a moist chamber on spots from
  24 July, after rain on 10-12 August, and even on 4 September in spots with weakened mycelium
  (after forcing for 2 or 3 days). "We think that the mycelium can keep its vitality in
  leaves through a dry summer, and if autumn is wet it can fruit and cause infection of the
  young autumn shoots" (l. 2289-2291).
- **Autumn shoots** (p. 83, l. 4691-4692): in some autumns the invasion is so vigorous that
  mycelium attacks green shoots even in mid-October.
- **In buds** (p. 84-85, l. 4765-4828): mycelium was found in summer buds (shoots collected
  July 1910 and 1912) and in winter buds collected at the end of October 1912
  (Stridó-Farkashegy); in attacked, completely closed buds they "always found" conidiophores
  and conidia, most between the outer scales, folded for lack of room. Infection of closed
  buds goes through the base of the eye by filaments from the cane. In winter buds
  infected, with a warm autumn the fungus forms conidiophores and many conidia (l. 4808-4810).
  Also stored 1910 material from Stridó-Farkashegy had well developed conidiophores inside
  closed winter eyes (p. 55, l. 3137-3139).
- **Cuboni's idea, cited via Viala** (l. 4800-4806, 4814-4817): mycelium overwinters in buds
  and invades the leaf in spring; Cuboni saw macroconidia under bud scales at the end of
  summer but did not follow spring development. The authors' own test was too small
  (l. 4817-4824): some buds on certainly attacked nodes grew, others did not, and it is not
  known whether the conidia in the latter were alive or how they might leave the bud. They
  note infected buds sit on the upper part of canes, so pruning could affect renewal of the
  disease (l. 4826-4828).
- **Earlier work** (l. 68-96): they list their own "Sur l'hivernage du Mildiou" (Növénytani
  Közlemények 3, 1904, pp. 74-77) and "Sur la perpétuation du Mildiou de la Vigne" (C. R.
  Acad. Sci. 138, 1904, pp. 643-644); not read here.
- **Greenhouse winter 1912/13** (p. 23-24, l. 1286-1310): the authors kept mildew going over
  winter on forced vines for serial inoculations; outdoors all leaves fall at the first white
  frost; Ravaz is cited as having had mildewed green shoots in the mild winter 1911/12.
  A fresh-conidia infection on 2 November 1912 of leaves 1 cm across worked (oil spots 9-10
  November); that was in a greenhouse.

**Not here:** no counts of oospores per area, no oospore germination test, and no
comparison of one autumn with the next spring's disease.

## Dependence

- **Own observations:** the 1911 and 1912 field dates, the buds and the winter-spore finds
  (Sántha is named for the cane mycelium, l. 4719-4722).
- **Cited:** Berlese (winter spores in green shoots), Cuboni (via Viala, "56 p. 106 à 107", for
  buds), Ravaz (green shoots in winter 1911/12), Müller-Thurgau (publications on incubation).
- **No formulation.** The incubation periods the authors derive (15-18 days in May) are set
  out in [istvanffi1913](istvanffi1913.md).

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data, Hungary 1910-1912:** winter spores were seen rarely, forming from mid-summer and
  not after autumn cooling; none in 800 spots from 125 places in late August 1911; the
  authors still tie the timing of the first spots to spring warmth and rain on winter spores
  (a judgement from two seasons' dates, with no germination observed).
- **Data, one winter:** mycelium can survive summer drought in spots and fruit in a wet
  autumn, and was found alive in closed winter buds; whether it gives spring spots is
  untested.
- **Opinion:** that oospores in berries and late shoots may matter in spring, and that first
  spots on lower leaves come from winter spores.
- Nothing links an autumn's late disease or winter weather to the size of the next spring's
  primary infections; they say only that the first invasion waits on spring warmth and rain.
