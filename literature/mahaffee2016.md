# Mahaffee & Stoll 2016: monitoring airborne pathogens for disease management

A review of how airborne inoculum is monitored and used in management decisions, and the limits of what inoculum data represent (l. 16-27). No record.

## What it holds

- **Samplers** (l. 61-65, 210-212, 267, 287-295, 576): air samplers usually take under 100 L per minute (range 10 to 1,000 L per minute by device). Passive samplers are cheap and need no power, so can be dense, but catch depends on air velocity and orientation to the wind; the rotorod (1.5 mm square rod) is most efficient above 15 micrometres (Noll 1970). Sensitivity depends on distance to the inoculum source and the air mass sampled (Savage et al. 2012). No catch-efficiency percentage and no number of traps per field or region is printed. It is not known whether air sampling beats scouting for early detection (l. 61); scouting finds aggregated disease less often than random disease (l. 74, no number).
- **Placement** (l. 528-563): largely an educated guess from pathosystem biology. The grape powdery mildew sampler was placed at plant scale where disease was worst the year before (Mahaffee 2014; Thiessen 2016, l. 541); where no overwintering is thought to occur, samplers go well above canopy height to cover more of the field (l. 554).
- **Mobile samplers** (l. 317-338): in simulation, mobile samplers on a 10 h flight detected a pathogen more often than passive vertical ones, with detection falling as source strength fell (Savage 2012). Copter and quadcopter flights over vineyards caught more than 300 E. necator spores; the plane only just above detection, probably because prop-wash disturbs the canopy (the authors' observation).
- **qPCR** (Table 1, Figure 3; the authors' unpublished data; l. 160, 256-259): a 25.1 kb amplicon gave Cq 32.4 +/- 1.8 without inhibitors and 42.5 +/- 5.0 with vineyard-soil inhibitors (dCq 10.2 +/- 3.6); a 3.7 kb fragment dCq 0.3 +/- 0.5. Standard curves: DNA y = -3.87x + 12.73, R2 0.992; spore extractions on rods y = -3.53x + 42.55, R2 0.996; lower limit 1 spore or 50 ITS copies. The "=" is printed as "5" in the text layer.
- **Wind and deposition** (l. 380, 468): wind speeds that start spore release are large (about 3 m/s; printed as ";3 m/s") against mean canopy speeds; turbulent fluctuations enhance deposition, and the mean wind in the Stokes number is wrong for glass slides (Moran et al. 2013).
- **Trap data and disease** (l. 88-93, 512-525): early work timed sprays from spore counts (Berger 1969, celery early blight with hours above 15 C; Harrison et al. 1965, potato early blight); reports tying airborne inoculum to severity conflict, and seasonal cumulative inoculum correlates with disease but may not predict it across regions (Carisse et al.). No thresholds printed.
- **Not printed:** a trap count, real-time detection times, any wetness threshold, any dispersal-kernel equation. Line 572 prints a 1 ha air volume as "1010 liters" (superscript lost); not used. Typos kept in cited names (Cerospora, Psuedoperonospora, "deceased" for decreased).

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

Strand 5 (sensor error) and strand 1 (autumn inoculum):

- **Trap as a sensor:** a spore count is a sample of a plume, with a catch that depends on trap type, wind and height, and a detection that falls with distance and source strength. For the sweep or for sensor placement, a trap model needs a catch efficiency by particle size, a footprint set by wind, and a detection limit (1 spore here), not a count taken as truth.
- **Placement:** the field's own practice is to put traps where disease was worst last year and above the canopy for coverage; there is no number to take for traps per hectare.
- **Limits:** light reading of a review; no quantitative efficiency or density; the qPCR figures are unpublished.
