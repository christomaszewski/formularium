# Papaïx et al. 2015: a simulation of crop pathogen emergence from wild patches

A demographic and genetic model of a pathogen living on wild host patches and a crop. It
asks which landscapes let the pathogen move onto the crop. Patches that are more aggregated
and cover more land help emergence, and a longer cropping season helps it too. No real
disease or field data are used.

## What it holds

- Model: a pathogen on one wild host, in patches that persist all year, and one crop
  grown in paddocks for part of the year (l. 24 to 30, model overview).
- Outcomes over the runs: extinction, wild specialist only, coexistence, or one generalist
  (Fig. 5). Extinction in 1.8% of simulations (l. 106).
- Landscape: more aggregated patches and more wild cover both raise wild pathogen numbers
  and speed emergence (l. 109 to 112). A longer cropping season speeds emergence and lowers
  pathogen dependence on the wild host (l. 115 to 116).
- Dispersal: a longer mean dispersal distance helps emergence and raises the crop's selection
  pressure (l. 122 to 123).

## Dependence

- Builds on the agro-ecological interface concept (Burdon and Thrall 2008) and on one
  earlier model of a virus with a wild reservoir (Fabre et al. 2012). No data taken from
  another study.

## Bearing (2026-10-20)

- Strand t6 (regional spread). The point that wild hosts bridge the off-season, and that
  patch layout sets the chance of a crop disease arising, is a general landscape term. A
  regional spread model could carry a wild-host reservoir between seasons.
- A generic model with one genotype per host: no vine, no disease, no parameter set to
  use. Not read in full.
