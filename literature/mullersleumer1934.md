# Müller & Sleumer 1934: biology of the vine mildew and the incubation calendar (abstract only)

An Italian reviewer's abstract of a German monograph, the source of the Baden incubation
calendar. The abstract gives only a fraction of the content.

## What it holds

- **Nature** (l. 12-14): a monograph on what the authors call the most harmful disease of
  the vine; the literature takes almost five pages.
- **Stomata and infection** (l. 16-23, reviewer's report of the authors' findings): leaves
  can be infected only once stomata are developed, when the blade is at least 2.5 x 3 cm;
  berries' stomata begin to degenerate at a diameter of 2.5 mm; at 7-9 mm the stalks have no
  open stomata and infection can occur only at the point where the stalk meets the fruit.
- **Control** (l. 25-28): aim copper sprays at the underside of leaves (no effective
  fungicide other than copper was found); the incubation calendar is stressed.
- **Primary infection** (l. 32-36, cut off): "for the primary infection by overwintering
  oospores a temperature of at least 11 °C is needed, a period of rain (a single isolated
  rain is not enough) that wets the soil and makes..." (the sentence ends there).

**Second-hand, not in the abstract:**
- Baldacci 1947 (l. 1235-1238 of its OCR) says Müller & Sleumer compared winter-spring rain
  and epidemics at some German stations and reached the same conclusion as his own test of
  Capus's scheme: wet winters do not by themselves bring severe epidemics.
- Grünzel 1960 and 1961 quote them that "the oospores are enormously frequent"
  ("daß die Oosporen ungeheuer häufig sind") and report oospores in quantity at the edge of
  infection spots, and from summer vines kept dry (Grünzel 1961 excerpt, l. 29-30, 58-59,
  64-66).

## Dependence

- No author is named in the abstract for any claim. The calendar is the Baden incubation
  calendar (Müller 1918, Müller 1922 and Müller & Rabanus 1923 are in Baldacci's list). Not
  computed or tested here.

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data (as abstracted):** a minimum of about 11 °C and a soil-wetting rainy period for
  oospore primary infection; the abstract says nothing of autumn or winter conditions.
- **Opinion/second-hand:** that oospores are "enormously frequent" in Baden (quoted by
  Grünzel), and that winter-spring rain alone did not predict epidemics at some stations
  (as read by Baldacci). Neither is checked here against the original.
