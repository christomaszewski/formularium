# Ravaz 1914: winter spores, and the failure of autumn conidia to overwinter

The chapter of Ravaz's treatise on mildew that sets out what survives the winter: conidia
(tested, and rejected), mycelium (rare), and the oospores ("spores d'hiver").

## What it holds

- **Autumn conidia** (p. 53-54, l. 1623-1693). Opinion first: sporangia-conidia are fragile,
  so their role is probably only in the good season; those formed in autumn are very
  numerous and fall near where they form (l. 1623-1631). Then the author's own test, autumn
  1912 to winter 1913 (l. 1634-1682): fresh conidia sown on fine silty-clay soil in a
  bottomless pot sunk in the vineyard (14 October; pot 1), on sterilised sand (21 October;
  pots 2 and 3) and sea sand (pot 4), and on soil, covered with 5 mm of soil (25 October;
  pot 5) or uncovered (pot 6). Soil or sand was washed into sterilised rain water in
  January and sprayed on Grenache and Aramon leaves: on 14 January a few yellow-brown
  conidia and zoospore-like swimmers were seen, and one Grenache leaf showed conidiophores on
  29 January. Every other trial (about eight listed, to 14 February) gave no result by 11
  February or 3 March.
- **His reading** (l. 1683-1693): except in one trial "which may be tainted with error", summer
  or autumn conidia do not seem to survive the winter in or on the soil; they rot or insects
  destroy them; so they "play no part in the invasions of the following spring". He adds
  that the question "should be taken up again".
- **Mycelium** (p. 55, l. 1698-1706): "the parasite shows itself every year, so it must have
  less fragile means of multiplication" (l. 1696-1697, inference). Fréchou, "under rather
  special conditions", established the survival of mycelium; cases are probably very rare;
  Baillon and Cuboni showed late partial invasion of a herbaceous bud can overwinter and
  grow with the young shoot; nature has not yet given clear cases, so this is "very
  chancy". Cited, not tested.
- **Winter spores: where and when they form** (p. 55, l. 1708-1719). In all invaded tissues
  but chiefly in leaves "when vine growth is much slowed, that is in August, September,
  October"; they seem to form "when the parasite is in an unfavourable medium". Also formed in
  late autumn in a greenhouse on young growing leaves, from sowing with late conidia; he
  guesses such germs may pass on a tendency to make winter spores. Opinion, with one
  observation.
- **Structure and survival** (p. 55-56, l. 1720-1763). Thick, hard-walled bodies; sexual
  origin as described by Prillieux (cited); Ravaz could not find antheridia, the oogonia being
  wrapped in many hyphae. Cornu is quoted that they can stay dry and shut from air and
  moisture "several months and even several years, above all if buried"; Ravaz: "nothing
  proves it is so". They pass the winter in the organs where they formed, on the soil
  surface or more or less buried; whether they resist several years is asked, not answered.
  "They germinate in spring when placed in suitable conditions" (p. 57, l. 1769-1770).
- **Germination: earlier failures** (p. 57-58, l. 1772-1860): Farlow, Cornu, Millardet,
  Fréchou, Viala, Richon and Prillieux, as in [ravazverge1913](ravazverge1913.md).
- **His own germination work** (p. 59-63, l. 1870-1990): "we took up the question again in
  1940-41" (printed; an OCR error for 1910-11, since the first germination is dated spring
  1911 at l. 1909-1910). Same method as the 1913 paper: leaves with mosaic spots picked "at
  the end of the season when the first frosts are about to occur", pieces left outdoors on
  the soil or buried at known depths, germinated in a warm greenhouse; winter spores "do not
  evolve" in winter; the macroconidium reaches full size in 2 h 55 (l. 1957-1961). The
  tests of germination in water and temperature follow (l. 2140-2214; not read in detail).
- **Not in pp. 53-58:** any count of oospores per area, any link between an autumn's disease
  or winter's weather and the next spring.

## Dependence

- Cites Farlow, Cornu, Millardet, Fréchou, Viala, Richon, Prillieux, Baillon, Cuboni and
  Gregory for earlier work (l. 1614, 1698-1701, 1729-1740, 1772-1869).
- The pot tests of autumn conidia and the winter-spore germination series are his own and his
  assistants' (Ravaz & Verge 1913, [ravazverge1913](ravazverge1913.md), is the paper form).
- No formulation is computed.

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data:** one winter of pot trials on autumn conidia gave no infection except one doubtful
  trial, so autumn conidia are not the carrier of spring invasions; this is evidence against
  one route from a diseased autumn to next spring (conidia), not against another (oospores).
- **Opinion:** winter spores form mostly in August-October when vine growth slows (the
  statement is written as what seems to occur); mycelium overwintering in buds is rare.
- The chapter treats oospores as the means by which "the parasite shows itself every year"
  but gives no figures for how many an autumn leaves or what sets their number.
