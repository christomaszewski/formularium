# Arens 1929: oospore germination and cytology (abstract only)

The reviewer's English abstract of Arens's long study of the oospores of Plasmopara viticola.
The original was not found. All statements below are the reviewer's report of Arens's own
experiments unless marked.

## What it holds

**Part (a), pp. 57-92** (abstract l. 5-60; the reviewer says the statistical data are in tables):
- **Where oospores form** (l. 16-19): almost exclusively in the intercostal areas of leaves;
  particularly many, "up to 250 per sq. mm.", in small mosaic-like infection spots; sometimes
  in narrow strips over a heavily infected leaf, probably tied to how the mycelium spreads
  between veins (l. 19-36). The older the leaf, the harder for the mycelium to cross the veins,
  hence the mosaic look in mature leaves (l. 34-36).
- **When** (l. 38-39): "Oospores develop not only in the autumn but also at a short interval
  after initial infection."
- **Germination** (l. 39-44): normally by a germ tube with a terminal conidium. It speeds up as
  spring advances: the period needed fell from "88" days for material laid in the germination
  bed on 21 March 1926 (the OCR; next to "4-4" and "2" days it is most likely 8.8, but this
  cannot be read from the text) to 4.4 days on 27 April and 2 days on 10 June.
- **Season** (l. 45-51): overwintered material germinated in the laboratory at any time between
  January and July; occasionally in December and very seldom in November; attempts in later
  summer on material kept outdoors since the previous autumn failed; "for some unknown reason
  certain individuals require a second winter to complete their resting period".
- **Temperature and moisture** (l. 53-57): germination between 13 and 33 °C, optimum 25 °C;
  "low temperatures curtail the resting period while dry conditions prolong it and may, if
  protracted, injure the oospores"; the primary conidia are "in all probability" splashed to
  the leaves by rain (the reviewer's wording of Arens's inference).
- **Cytology** (l. 59-60): described and compared with other workers; not detailed.

**Part (b), pp. 93-157** (l. 63-71): only the title, the sentence that attempts to culture
the fungus on artificial media failed, and the start of "a very full study" survive in the
OCR; nothing on infection conditions can be read.

**Cited by others (second-hand, Grünzel 1961 excerpt, not seen in the abstract):**
- Oospores visible 2-4 weeks after infection, always bounded by the veins (l. 48-52);
  250 per mm² counted (l. 54-55); found everywhere in the Franconian wine area in 1923-1926
  (l. 27-28); Arens and Müller & Sleumer got oospores in summer from infected vines or leaves
  kept dry, but the main mass in the vineyard was found only in autumn (l. 64-66).
- Baldacci 1947 takes from Arens the germination method and a laboratory minimum of 13 °C
  (l. 842-843, 972 of its OCR).

## Dependence

- Compares his results "with those of other workers" in the cytology (abstract l. 59-60); no
  names are in the abstract. No formulation computed or tested.
- Referred to as "Arens 1926" in some held notes: no such paper; 1926 is the date of the start
  of his germination series (21 March 1926).

## Bearing (2026-10-18)

- **Data (as abstracted):** oospores can form within weeks of infection, not only in autumn;
  germination in the laboratory runs January to July, is faster as spring advances, needs
  13-33 °C, and is shortened by cold and prolonged by dryness; some oospores need a second
  winter. So winter weather could set the date and speed of germination, and the abstract
  mentions no data on numbers per season.
- **Not in the abstract:** any link between an autumn's late disease, or winter weather, and
  the strength of next spring's primary infections.
